Question 1 · severe CAP, electrolytes & coverage
I'm treating severe community-acquired pneumonia and the patient's now hyponatremic (Na 124) with diarrhea and a mild transaminitis — does that constellation specifically point to Legionella, and should it change my empiric coverage?
Grounded reasoningThe composed answer, built only from the grounded steps below and joined by the relations the corpus supports.
Legionella pneumonia is classically associated with hyponatremia [Clinical Manifestations and Diagnosis of Legionella Infection] and is classically associated with gastrointestinal symptoms like diarrhea [same], therefore standard empiric therapy for severe community-acquired pneumonia typically includes coverage for Legionella [Treatment and Prevention of Legionella Infection].
Reasoning from the cited evidenceThe labeled inference — the decision the cited premises support. Reasoning, not a citation.

The presence of hyponatremia and gastrointestinal symptoms is consistent with Legionella pneumonia, which is already addressed by standard empiric therapy for severe community-acquired pneumonia.

Grounded steps (cited)Each step: the claim, the source it was quoted from, and the verbatim sentence that supports it.
✓ Legionella pneumonia is classically associated with hyponatremia. [Clinical Manifestations and Diagnosis of Legionella Infection]

“Hyponatremia (serum sodium mEq/L) occurs significantly more frequently in Legionnaires' disease than in pneumonias of other etiologies.”

✓ Legionella pneumonia is classically associated with gastrointestinal symptoms like diarrhea. [Clinical Manifestations and Diagnosis of Legionella Infection]

“Gastrointestinal symptoms are often prominent with diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain.”

✓ Standard empiric therapy for severe community-acquired pneumonia typically includes coverage for Legionella. [Treatment and Prevention of Legionella Infection]

“…clinical signs of pneumonia will precede definitive laboratory diagnosis of Legionella in most patients, necessitating empiric coverage for many microorganisms, including Legionella.”

Question 3 · age-adjusted test interpretation
Low suspicion for PE (Wells 1) but the D-dimer is mildly above cutoff in a 72-year-old — can I apply an age-adjusted D-dimer to avoid CTPA?
Grounded reasoning
The age-adjusted D-dimer threshold is calculated as age × 10 ng/mL for patients older than 50 [Acute Pulmonary Embolism: Diagnosis and Management], therefore a 72-year-old has an age-adjusted cutoff of 720 ng/mL [same]. The threshold is validated in patients with low clinical probability of PE [Diagnosis of the Nonpregnant Adult with Suspected Acute PE], so if the D-dimer is below the age-adjusted threshold, PE can be safely ruled out without CTPA [same].
Reasoning from the cited evidence

On the cited evidence, an age-adjusted D-dimer cutoff can be applied to rule out PE without CTPA when the value falls below the age-adjusted threshold.

Grounded steps (cited)
✓ The age-adjusted D-dimer threshold is age × 10 ng/mL for patients over 50. [Acute Pulmonary Embolism: Diagnosis and Management]

“The formula to calculate age-adjusted D-dimer is Age (if over 50 years) × 10 = cutoff value in ng/mL (fibrinogen equivalent units).”

✓ The threshold is validated in patients with low clinical probability of PE. [Diagnosis of the Nonpregnant Adult with Suspected Acute PE]

“Among the 1141 untreated patients in whom PE was excluded by clinical assessment plus age-adjusted D-dimer testing, there were only two cases (0.2 percent) of nonfatal PE.”

✓ A 72-year-old has an age-adjusted D-dimer cutoff of 720 ng/mL. [Acute Pulmonary Embolism: Diagnosis and Management]

“…Age (if over 50 years) × 10 = cutoff value in ng/mL…” (72 × 10 = 720)

✓ If the D-dimer is below the age-adjusted threshold, PE can be safely ruled out without CTPA. [Diagnosis of the Nonpregnant Adult with Suspected Acute PE]

“…PE was excluded by clinical assessment plus age-adjusted D-dimer testing… only two cases (0.2 percent) of nonfatal PE.”

Scrub typhus can present without an eschar, and the absence of this finding does not rule out the diagnosis.

Question 6 · a time-critical can't-miss with normal markers

Giant cell arteritis can present with normal ESR and CRP [Blood Biomarkers in Large-Vessel Vasculitides] and the prevalence of biopsy-proven GCA with normal inflammatory markers is non-zero [Diagnostic Challenges and Therapeutic Strategies in GCA], so normal ESR/CRP does not rule out GCA and does not preclude temporal artery biopsy [Diagnosis of Giant Cell Arteritis], therefore clinical suspicion of GCA with jaw claudication and visual symptoms warrants immediate high-dose corticosteroids regardless of ESR/CRP [Neuro-ophthalmic Manifestations of GCA].
Reasoning from the cited evidence

On the cited evidence, normal inflammatory markers do not exclude giant cell arteritis; immediate high-dose corticosteroids and temporal artery biopsy are supported despite the normal ESR/CRP.

Grounded steps (cited)
✓ GCA can present with normal ESR and CRP. [Blood Biomarkers in Large-Vessel Vasculitides]

“…in a cohort of 177 patients with biopsy-proven GCA, eighteen (10.2%) showed normal values of both ESR and CRP… at the time of diagnosis.”

✓ The prevalence of biopsy-proven GCA with normal markers is non-zero. [Diagnostic Challenges and Therapeutic Strategies in GCA]

Question 7 · which antibody discriminates
Years on hydralazine, now arthralgias + fatigue + positive ANA — how do I separate drug-induced lupus from SLE, and which antibody settles it?
Grounded reasoning
Hydralazine is a known cause of drug-induced lupus (DILE) [Drug-Induced Lupus], whereas renal and CNS involvement are common in idiopathic SLE but rare in DILE [same]. DILE is strongly associated with anti-histone antibodies [Drug-Induced Lupus], whereas SLE is typically associated with anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith antibodies [Diagnosis and Differential Diagnosis of SLE in Adults].
Reasoning from the cited evidence

On the cited evidence, drug-induced lupus is distinguished from SLE by the antibody profile — anti-histone in DILE versus anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith in SLE — and by the absence of renal and central nervous system involvement in DILE.

Grounded steps (cited)
✓ Hydralazine is a known cause of drug-induced lupus. [Drug-Induced Lupus]

“Medications identified as definitely causing drug-induced lupus include procainamide, hydralazine, minocycline, diltiazem, penicillamine, isoniazid, quinidine, anti-TNF-alpha therapy…”

✓ DILE is strongly associated with anti-histone antibodies. [Drug-Induced Lupus]

“Anti-histone antibodies are strongly associated with some forms of drug-induced lupus.”

✓ SLE is typically associated with anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith antibodies. [Diagnosis and Differential Diagnosis of SLE in Adults]

“…some antibodies (eg, anti-double-stranded DNA [dsDNA] and anti-Smith [Sm]) highly associated with this condition.”

✓ Renal and CNS involvement are common in idiopathic SLE but rare in DILE. [Drug-Induced Lupus]

“…more severe manifestations, such as kidney disease and central nervous system involvement, are uncommon, although they may occur.”

Ovarian cancer is a particularly high-risk malignancy in women with dermatomyositis [Dermatomyositis: Prevalence, Clinical Spectrum, and Management].
Reasoning from the cited evidence

On the cited evidence, a new diagnosis of dermatomyositis supports age-appropriate cancer screening, with ovarian cancer a particular concern in women.

Grounded steps (cited)
✓ Dermatomyositis is associated with increased malignancy risk. [Cutaneous Manifestations of Internal Malignancy]

“Adults with dermatomyositis have increased risk for malignancy.”

✓ Age-appropriate cancer screening is recommended at new diagnosis. [Dermatomyositis: Skin as a Window to Muscle and Malignancy]
